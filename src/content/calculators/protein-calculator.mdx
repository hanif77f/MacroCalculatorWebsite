# Quick Answer: Daily Protein Target

Calculate a practical daily protein target based on your body size, activity, and goal. This calculator gives you a starting point for deciding how much protein to aim for each day, whether you are focused on general nutrition, weight loss, or building muscle.

Protein needs are not identical for everyone. A person who is sedentary, someone who trains several days a week, and someone trying to build muscle may have different targets. The result is therefore best treated as a useful starting estimate rather than a fixed rule.

---

# How Much Protein Do You Need?

There is no single protein number that works for everyone. Your needs depend on factors such as body weight, age, activity level, training, and the reason you are increasing or maintaining your protein intake.

For healthy adults, the U.S. Recommended Dietary Allowance is 0.8 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight per day. That is a population-level baseline, not a universal target for athletes or people pursuing a specific training goal. ([1](https://nap.nationalacademies.org/catalog/10490/dietary-reference-intakes-for-energy-carbohydrate-fiber-fat-fatty-acids))

For people who exercise regularly, sports-nutrition guidance commonly uses higher intakes. The International Society of Sports Nutrition states that 1.4–2.0 g/kg/day is sufficient for most exercising individuals, while research in resistance training has found benefits level off around 1.6 g/kg/day for many healthy adults. ([2](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-017-0177-8), [3](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2017-097608))

> These ranges are starting points rather than guarantees. The calculator helps you choose a reasonable target; it does not establish an exact medical requirement.

### A practical reference range

| Situation | Common reference | 70 kg example |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| General adult baseline | 0.8 g/kg/day | 56 g/day |
| Active adult | 1.2–2.0 g/kg/day | 84–140 g/day |
| Exercising / resistance training | 1.4–2.0 g/kg/day | 98–140 g/day |
| Muscle-building starting point | Around 1.6 g/kg/day | 112 g/day |

The table is a reference for healthy adults, not a prescription. Individual needs can differ.

# What Does Protein Do?

Protein supplies amino acids that the body uses to build and repair tissues and to make enzymes, hormones, and other proteins. It is found throughout the body, including in muscle, skin, and other tissues.

Protein also plays a practical role in everyday eating. Meals that contain a meaningful protein source can make it easier to meet a daily target without relying on a single large serving at the end of the day.

# Protein Intake Formula

When protein needs are expressed per kilogram of body weight, the basic calculation is straightforward:

**Core formula**

**Protein target (g/day) = Body weight (kg) × Protein target (g/kg/day)**

For example, a 75 kg person using a target of 1.6 g/kg/day would calculate:

**Example**

**75 × 1.6 = 120 g protein/day**

When the target is given per pound, the same idea applies using body weight in pounds. Keep the units consistent because g/kg and g/lb are different scales.

---

# Protein Per Kilogram vs Protein Per Pound

Protein recommendations are often written in grams per kilogram of body weight, while U.S.-based fitness content frequently uses grams per pound. They describe the same kind of target, but the numerical values are different because a kilogram contains 2.205 pounds.

| Reference | Equivalent | Example for 70 kg / 154.3 lb |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| 0.8 g/kg | 0.36 g/lb | 56 g/day |
| 1.2 g/kg | 0.54 g/lb | 84 g/day |
| 1.6 g/kg | 0.73 g/lb | 112 g/day |
| 2.0 g/kg | 0.91 g/lb | 140 g/day |
| 2.2 g/kg | 1.00 g/lb | 154 g/day |

The pound equivalents are rounded for practical use. Avoid presenting them as more precise than the underlying recommendation.

# Protein for Different Goals

The same person may choose different protein targets at different stages of training or dieting. The goal does not completely determine protein needs, but it helps explain why a range can be more useful than a single universal number.

## Protein for Weight Loss

During [weight loss](/calculators/calorie-deficit-calculator), getting enough protein can help support the retention of lean tissue while calories are reduced. Resistance training and an appropriately planned diet also matter, so protein should be viewed as one part of the overall plan rather than a guarantee of a particular outcome.

The calculator selects a range using both activity and goal. Weight-loss targets rise above the general adult baseline as activity increases; see the range table in “How the Protein Calculator Works” for the method used. These are planning estimates for healthy adults, not individualized prescriptions.

## Protein for Muscle Gain

Building muscle requires training as well as enough energy and protein. For healthy adults who perform resistance training, research and sports-nutrition guidance commonly support protein intakes around 1.4–2.0 g/kg/day, with evidence suggesting that gains in [lean mass](/calculators/lean-body-mass-calculator) often level off around 1.6 g/kg/day for many people. ([2](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-017-0177-8), [3](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2017-097608))

That does not mean a higher number is automatically better. Total energy intake, progressive resistance training, sleep, and consistency all contribute to the result.

## Protein for Maintaining Muscle

Maintenance does not mean protein stops mattering. People who are active, dieting, or getting older may have different needs from the basic adult RDA. The useful target depends on the person and the context, so the calculator provides a sensible starting point rather than a rigid maximum or minimum.

Body weight is one of the simplest ways to scale a protein recommendation. A target expressed in grams per kilogram automatically increases as body weight increases and decreases as body weight decreases.

# Protein Needs Through Different Life Stages

Protein requirements are influenced by age and physiological circumstances. The standard adult RDA is 0.8 g/kg/day, while some research and expert groups recommend higher intakes for healthy older adults to support muscle and functional health. ([1](https://nap.nationalacademies.org/catalog/10490/dietary-reference-intakes-for-energy-carbohydrate-fiber-fat-fatty-acids), [4](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23867520/))

* **Adults:** The standard U.S. RDA is 0.8 g/kg/day for adults.
* **Older adults:** Several expert groups have suggested at least 1.0–1.2 g/kg/day for healthy older adults, with context and health status influencing the appropriate amount. ([4](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23867520/))
* **Pregnancy and lactation:** Protein needs change during these life stages. A general-purpose fitness calculator should not substitute for individualized guidance from a qualified clinician or dietitian.

# High-Protein Foods

Once you know your daily target, the practical question becomes how to reach it. Protein can come from animal foods, dairy, eggs, legumes, soy foods, nuts, seeds, and other plant foods. Variety can make a high-protein diet easier to sustain and can also improve the overall nutrient quality of meals.

| Food | Serving example | Approx. protein* |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| Chicken breast | 100 g cooked | 31 g |
| Tuna | 100 g | 29 g |

| Greek yogurt | 170 g | 17 g |

| Lentils | 1 cup cooked | ~18 g |
| Milk | 1 cup | ~8 g |

*Protein values vary by product, serving size, cooking method, and database. Treat food values as approximate and use the nutrition label or a reliable food database for precise tracking.*

### Building a Protein-Friendly Day

A daily protein target is easier to manage when it is spread across ordinary meals. For example, someone aiming for 120 g might build breakfast around eggs and yogurt, have chicken or tofu at lunch, include fish or lean meat at dinner, and use milk, Greek yogurt, beans, or another protein-rich snack to close the gap.

The goal is not to make every meal identical. A consistent pattern that gets reasonably close to the daily target is more useful than chasing a perfect number at every sitting.

# Complete and Incomplete Proteins

Protein foods differ in their amino-acid profiles. Animal foods and some plant foods provide all of the essential amino acids in sufficient proportions and are often described as complete proteins. Other plant foods may be lower in one or more essential amino acids relative to human requirements.

This does not mean plant-based diets cannot provide adequate protein. A varied diet that includes beans, lentils, soy foods, grains, nuts, and seeds can contribute substantial protein across the day. The overall pattern matters more than treating one meal as a pass-or-fail test.

---

# Protein Timing and Meal Distribution

Total daily protein is the first number to get right. For people who train, spreading protein across meals can also be a practical way to support muscle protein synthesis and make the daily target easier to reach. Sports-nutrition guidance commonly discusses roughly 20–40 g of high-quality protein per serving, or about 0.25 g/kg, although the ideal amount varies with body size, age, and training context. ([5](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-017-0177-8))

You do not need to treat a workout like a deadline for protein. A normal meal or snack around training can contribute to the day’s total, and the exact timing matters less than consistently reaching an appropriate overall intake.

# How Many Calories Are in Protein?

Protein provides 4 calories per gram. This is useful when you are connecting a protein target to a broader calorie or [macro plan](/).

**Protein calories = Protein grams × 4**

For example, 120 g of protein contributes about 480 calories. ([6](https://www.nal.usda.gov/human-nutrition-and-food-safety/food-composition))

# Common Protein Intake Mistakes

## Treating One Number as Universal

Protein needs differ. A baseline adult recommendation and a target for someone doing regular resistance training are not necessarily the same.

## Confusing the Protein Target With a Calorie Target

Protein is one part of a diet. Meeting a protein target does not automatically tell you how many calories, carbohydrates, or fats you should consume.

## Forgetting the Units

A recommendation stated as grams per kilogram cannot be applied directly to pounds. Convert the body weight first or use a pound-based recommendation.

## Chasing Perfect Meal Numbers

It is easy to turn protein tracking into a search for perfect numbers at every meal. A reasonable daily pattern is usually more useful than making one meal carry the whole target.

## Relying Only on Supplements

Protein powders can be convenient, but they are not required to meet protein needs. Ordinary foods can supply protein while also contributing vitamins, minerals, fiber, and other nutrients.

## Ignoring the Rest of the Diet

A high-protein diet can still be low in fiber or micronutrients if food variety is poor. Protein should fit into an overall balanced eating pattern.

# How Accurate Is a Protein Calculator?

A protein calculator is a planning tool, not a laboratory test. Its output depends on the recommendation or evidence range built into the calculator and on the accuracy of the information the user enters.

For healthy adults, a body-weight-based estimate can be useful because body weight is easy to measure and protein guidance is commonly expressed in grams per kilogram. But different goals and populations can call for different targets, and research does not support pretending that one exact number is right for every person.

> The result is an estimated daily protein target or starting protein target, not an exact personal requirement.

---

# When Should You Recalculate Your Protein Needs?

Recalculate when your body weight changes meaningfully, your training changes, or your goal changes. Someone who moves from a sedentary routine into regular resistance training may choose a different protein range, just as someone coming out of a dieting phase may adjust their target.

Small day-to-day changes do not require constant recalculation. Think in terms of meaningful changes in body weight, training, or goals rather than chasing daily fluctuations.

---

# Protein Needs for Women and Men

For most healthy adults, protein recommendations are primarily expressed in relation to body weight, activity, age, and goal rather than by creating completely separate “men’s” and “women’s” protein rules. Sex can be one contextual factor, but it does not replace the need to consider the individual’s size and activity.

That is why the calculator lets people enter relevant body measurements and goals rather than assigning a protein number from sex alone.

# Who Should Use This Calculator?

This calculator is intended as a general educational tool for healthy adults who want a practical starting point for daily protein intake.

People with kidney disease, significant medical conditions affecting nutrition, pregnancy or breastfeeding, a history of eating disorders, or a medically prescribed diet should seek individualized guidance from an appropriate healthcare professional or registered dietitian rather than relying on a general calculator.

# Frequently Asked Questions

## What is a protein calculator?

A protein calculator estimates a practical daily protein target from information such as body weight, activity, and goal. It provides a starting point rather than a laboratory measurement of your personal protein requirement.

## How much protein do I need per day?

It depends on your body weight, activity, age, and goal. The standard adult RDA is 0.8 g/kg/day, while active and resistance-trained adults often use higher ranges.

## How much protein should I eat?

Start with a target appropriate for your body weight and circumstances, then use the result as a daily planning guide rather than a rigid number.

## How do I calculate my protein needs?

A simple weight-based calculation is body weight in kilograms multiplied by a protein target in grams per kilogram per day.

## How much protein should I eat to lose weight?

A higher protein intake can be useful during weight loss, particularly alongside resistance training, but the appropriate target depends on the person. Use the calculator as a starting point and adjust with context.

## How much protein do I need to build muscle?

For people doing resistance training, evidence-based guidance commonly supports about 1.4–2.0 g/kg/day, with many studies finding that benefits level off around 1.6 g/kg/day for healthy adults.

## How much protein should I eat after a workout?

You do not need to hit an exact post-workout deadline. A protein-containing meal or snack around training can contribute to your daily total, and total daily intake is the larger consideration.

## How much protein is in a gram of protein?

One gram of protein provides about 4 calories.

## How much protein is too much?

There is no single cutoff that applies to every healthy adult. Very high intakes are not automatically better, and people with certain medical conditions may need individualized limits. Discuss unusually high targets with a qualified professional.

## Should I calculate protein by kilograms or pounds?

Either can work, but use the unit that matches the recommendation. Do not apply a grams-per-kilogram value directly to a body weight entered in pounds.

## Do women need a different protein calculator?

The main calculation should account for body size, activity, age, and goal rather than treating women as a completely separate category. Some life stages require additional context and individualized advice.

## Do men need more protein than women?

Not automatically. Protein needs scale largely with body weight and other individual factors. Two people of different sexes can have similar protein targets when their circumstances are similar.

## Can I get enough protein from food without supplements?

Yes. Many people can meet their target from ordinary foods such as dairy, eggs, meat, fish, soy foods, legumes, nuts, and seeds.

## Is a protein calculator accurate?

It can provide a useful starting estimate, but it cannot measure your personal protein requirement directly. The result depends on the method used and the information entered.

## How often should I recalculate my protein target?

Recalculate when your body weight, training routine, or goal changes meaningfully. There is usually no need to recalculate because of small daily fluctuations.
