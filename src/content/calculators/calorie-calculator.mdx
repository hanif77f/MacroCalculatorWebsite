# Quick Answer: How Many Calories Should I Eat Per Day?

There is no single calorie number that fits everyone. Daily calorie needs depend on factors including age, sex, height, weight, metabolism, and physical activity. A calorie calculator estimates your needs from those inputs and can then adjust the estimate according to a weight-maintenance, weight-loss, or weight-gain goal. NIDDK explains that calorie needs differ with age, weight, height, sex, metabolism, and activity level.

For a healthy adult, the calculator result is best used as a starting point. Real-world calorie needs can be higher or lower than a formula predicts, so your weight trend, hunger, energy, and activity should be considered over time.

# What Is a Calorie?

A food Calorie is a unit of energy. In nutrition, the word Calorie with a capital C means a kilocalorie (kcal). Calories represent the energy available from food and beverages and the energy your body uses to support basic functions, movement, digestion, exercise, and other physiological processes.

Most nutrition labels report energy in Calories, while scientific sources may use kilocalories or kilojoules. The calculator can display either Calories or kilojoules without changing the underlying estimate.

# How the Calorie Calculator Works

The calculator estimates your resting energy expenditure first, then accounts for typical physical activity. The resulting estimate is used as a practical daily calorie requirement. The same basic framework is used by many established calorie calculators: estimate resting energy from body measurements, apply an activity multiplier, and then adjust the result for a goal.

The calculator should default to Mifflin-St Jeor for adults because it is a widely used predictive equation for resting energy expenditure. Calculator.net also offers Revised Harris-Benedict and Katch-McArdle as alternative methods. The original Mifflin-St Jeor study was published in the *American Journal of Clinical Nutrition* in 1990.

# Step 1: Estimate Resting Energy Expenditure

The first stage estimates how much energy your body uses at rest. This is commonly called basal metabolic rate (BMR) or resting energy expenditure depending on the equation and context.

Our calculator can expose the formula choice so advanced users can see how different methods affect the result. Most users can leave the default Mifflin-St Jeor method selected.

## Mifflin-St Jeor Equation

**Mifflin-St Jeor — Men**

**BMR = 10W + 6.25H − 5A + 5**

W = weight in kg; H = height in cm; A = age in years

**Mifflin-St Jeor — Women**

**BMR = 10W + 6.25H − 5A − 161**

W = weight in kg; H = height in cm; A = age in years

## Revised Harris-Benedict Equation

The Revised Harris-Benedict equations provide another estimate of resting energy needs. They are older than Mifflin-St Jeor but remain widely recognized and are useful as an alternative comparison method.

**Revised Harris-Benedict — Men**

**BMR = 13.397W + 4.799H − 5.677A + 88.362**

W = kg; H = cm; A = years

**Revised Harris-Benedict — Women**

**BMR = 9.247W + 3.098H − 4.330A + 447.593**

W = kg; H = cm; A = years

## Katch-McArdle Formula

Katch-McArdle uses lean body mass rather than total body weight. This makes it especially relevant when a user has a reasonably reliable body-fat estimate and wants a body-composition-based resting energy estimate.

**Katch-McArdle**

**BMR = 370 + 21.6 × LBM**

LBM = lean body mass in kg

Because this method depends on lean body mass, an inaccurate body-fat or lean-mass estimate can carry directly into the calorie result. Use the [Body Fat Calculator](/calculators/body-fat-calculator) and [Lean Body Mass Calculator](/calculators/lean-body-mass-calculator) as supporting tools rather than treating a consumer estimate as a direct measurement.

# Step 2: Account for Physical Activity

Your body uses more energy than it would at complete rest because you move, work, exercise, stand, walk, and digest food. The calculator accounts for typical activity by applying an activity multiplier to the resting estimate.

**Daily calorie requirement**

**Estimated daily calories = BMR × activity factor**

The activity factor should match the levels implemented in the live calculator.

| Activity level | Typical description | Common use |
|---|---|---|
| Sedentary | Little or no structured exercise; mostly sitting | Lower activity estimate |
| Lightly active | Light exercise or activity several days per week | Desk-based lifestyle with regular movement |
| Moderately active | Moderate exercise or active routine most days | Regular training and daily movement |
| Very active | Hard training most days or a physically demanding routine | High activity |
| Extra active | Very hard training and/or highly physical work | Highest activity estimate |

Activity levels are estimates, not precise measurements. NIDDK notes that physical activity level changes the number of calories a person needs and its Body Weight Planner uses a physical-activity-level framework for individualized planning.

# Step 3: Estimate Maintenance Calories

Maintenance calories are the approximate amount of energy you need to keep your body weight relatively stable under your current routine. The calculator estimates this by combining your resting energy estimate with your typical activity.

**Maintenance calories**

**Maintenance calories ≈ BMR × activity factor**

This is an estimate of energy balance, not a guarantee of weight stability.

If your actual intake and expenditure remain different from the estimate, your body weight may rise or fall over time. The purpose of the calculator is to provide a reasonable starting point for planning rather than to predict an exact daily number.

# Step 4: Adjust Calories for Your Goal

Once maintenance calories are estimated, the calculator can show practical targets for weight loss or weight gain. The size of the adjustment should be treated as a planning choice rather than a prediction of exactly how much body weight will change.

| Goal | Calculation concept | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Maintain weight | Maintenance calories | Starting estimate for weight stability |
| Mild weight loss | Maintenance − modest deficit | Smaller reduction for a slower approach |
| Weight loss | Maintenance − larger deficit | Larger reduction, still within a sensible plan |
| Mild weight gain | Maintenance + modest surplus | Small increase above maintenance |
| Weight gain | Maintenance + larger surplus | Larger increase above maintenance |

NIDDK recommends using sustainable eating and activity strategies for weight management rather than relying on a single calculator number. Its Body Weight Planner is designed to personalize calorie and activity plans around a goal and timeframe.

# Calorie Calculator for Weight Loss

A calorie calculator for weight loss starts with an estimate of maintenance calories and then creates a [calorie deficit](/calculators/calorie-deficit-calculator). A deficit means consuming less energy than the body uses over time.

The calculator should present weight-loss calories as a starting range or practical target, not as a promise that a particular amount of weight will be lost every week. Actual weight change is affected by changes in body mass, water, activity, appetite, and energy expenditure.

**Important**

Avoid presenting a simple calorie deficit as an exact forecast of weekly fat loss. The relationship between calorie intake and body-weight change is dynamic, which is why the NIH Body Weight Planner uses a more detailed model for goal planning.

# Calorie Calculator for Weight Gain

For weight gain, the calculator can add calories above estimated maintenance. A modest surplus is often easier to adjust than a very large increase because appetite, training, and changes in body composition vary between people.

The goal should be to use the calculator as a starting point, monitor the trend over several weeks, and adjust intake when real-world results do not match the intended direction.

# How Many Calories Should I Eat to Maintain My Weight?

Your maintenance calories are the approximate amount of energy required to keep your weight stable under your current activity pattern. The most useful maintenance estimate is personalized rather than based only on an average calorie number.

Public dietary guidance gives broad reference ranges for adults, but those ranges are not substitutes for an individual estimate. The Dietary Guidelines for Americans notes that adult calorie needs vary with age, sex, and activity and gives broad reference levels rather than one universal target.

# Calories for Men and Women

Calorie needs differ among individuals of every sex, but population-level estimates generally show higher average energy requirements for men than women because average body size and body composition differ. A personalized calculator should use the user's own measurements rather than assigning a generic number.

## Calories for Men

For men, the calculator uses age, height, weight, and activity to estimate daily energy needs. The result is individualized rather than based on the average male calorie requirement.

## Calories for Women

For women, the calculator uses the same broad input categories but applies the appropriate sex-specific equation. Individual needs still vary widely with body size, activity, and other factors.

# Calories by Age

Calorie requirements generally decline with age at the population level, partly because resting energy expenditure and activity can change. Older adults can still have substantially different calorie needs from one another depending on body size and physical activity.

| Life stage | General pattern | Why individual estimates matter |
|---|---|---|
| Younger adults | Often higher energy needs | Body size and activity can vary widely |
| Middle adulthood | Needs may gradually change | Activity and body composition become more important |
| Older adulthood | Average needs tend to decline | Lower resting expenditure and activity may contribute |

The Dietary Guidelines report that calorie needs generally decrease with age and vary with sex and activity.

# Calories and Activity Level

Two people with the same height and weight can have different calorie needs because their daily movement and exercise are different. Activity level should represent your normal routine rather than your most active week.

## Choosing the Right Activity Level

- Think about your usual week, including work, walking, exercise, and daily movement.
- Choose the lower category when you are between two levels and are unsure.
- Compare your real-world weight trend with the estimate over several weeks.
- Adjust the calorie target when your routine changes significantly.

# Calories and BMR

BMR is the resting-energy component of the calculation. It is not your total daily calorie requirement because it does not include the energy you use through normal movement and activity.

A person can have a BMR of one value and still need substantially more calories across a normal day. This distinction is why the calculator should show BMR and daily calories as separate results.

# Calories and TDEE

TDEE stands for total daily energy expenditure. In common calculator workflows, TDEE represents the total amount of energy used across an entire day and is closely related to the maintenance-calorie estimate produced from BMR and activity.

The dedicated TDEE Calculator should own the deeper TDEE topic. The Calorie Calculator should use the concept to answer the user's practical question: how many calories should I eat each day?

**Topic boundary**

Do not turn the Calorie Calculator into a second full TDEE guide. Explain the relationship briefly, calculate the daily target, and link to the [TDEE Calculator](/calculators/tdee-calculator) for deeper methodology.

# How Accurate Is a Calorie Calculator?

A calorie calculator provides an estimate, not a direct measurement of energy expenditure. Predictive equations are useful because they require only basic measurements, but individual metabolism and daily movement can differ from a formula's assumptions.

## Why Results Can Differ From Real Needs

- Activity may be higher or lower than the selected category.
- Body-composition differences are not fully captured by all equations.
- Daily movement outside exercise can change from week to week.
- Energy expenditure can change as body weight changes.
- Food intake can be under- or overestimated when portions are not measured.

NIDDK likewise describes calorie needs as estimates and provides a separate Body Weight Planner for more individualized goal planning.

# Real-Life Calorie Calculation Examples

## Worked Example: 30-Year-Old Man

Suppose a 30-year-old man weighs 80 kg and is 175 cm tall. Using the Mifflin-St Jeor equation:

**Step 1 — BMR**

**10(80) + 6.25(175) − 5(30) + 5 = 1,749 kcal/day**

Rounded to the nearest calorie.

If the selected activity factor is 1.55, the maintenance estimate is:

**Step 2 — Maintenance**

**1,749 × 1.55 ≈ 2,711 kcal/day**

Starting maintenance estimate.

A practical weight-loss target could then be set below maintenance, while a weight-gain target could be set above maintenance. The calculator should apply the site's selected adjustment rather than presenting the example as a universal prescription.

## Worked Example: 30-Year-Old Woman

Suppose a 30-year-old woman weighs 65 kg and is 165 cm tall. Using Mifflin-St Jeor:

**Step 1 — BMR**

**10(65) + 6.25(165) − 5(30) − 161 = 1,370 kcal/day**

Rounded to the nearest calorie.

With an activity factor of 1.55:

**Step 2 — Maintenance**

**1,370 × 1.55 ≈ 2,124 kcal/day**

Starting maintenance estimate.

# How Many Calories Are in a Pound?

The traditional rule of thumb has often treated a 3,500-calorie energy difference as roughly equivalent to one pound of body weight. This can be useful as a simple historical approximation, but it should not be presented as an exact biological conversion or a guaranteed weekly weight change.

NIDDK's Body Weight Planner is based on a dynamic mathematical model rather than a fixed 3,500-calorie rule, reflecting the fact that energy expenditure changes as body weight changes.

# How to Track Calories

Tracking calories can help some people understand portion sizes and compare intake with a target. It is not required for everyone, and food-label rounding and portion-estimation errors mean that tracking is never perfectly precise.

1. Choose a tracking method you can use consistently.
2. Measure or weigh frequently eaten foods until portion sizes become familiar.
3. Read the serving size on packaged foods instead of assuming the entire package is one serving.
4. Look at weekly patterns rather than reacting to one unusually high or low day.

The goal is a useful picture of your average intake, not mathematical perfection.

# Common Calorie Calculation Mistakes

## Using the Wrong Units

Entering pounds where kilograms are expected, or centimeters where inches are expected, can produce a large error. The interface should make units obvious and switch fields cleanly when users change between US and Metric.

## Choosing an Unrealistic Activity Level

Picking the highest activity level because you exercise occasionally can overestimate daily calorie needs. Select the level that best represents your usual routine.

## Confusing BMR With Daily Calories

BMR is a resting estimate. It is not the same as the total calories you need to support a normal day of movement and activity.

## Treating the Result as Exact

Formula-based calorie estimates are starting points. The number may need adjustment when real-world weight trends consistently differ from the expected direction.

## Ignoring Changes in Body Weight

Calorie needs can change as body weight and activity change. A target that worked several months ago may not be the right starting point today.

# When Should You Recalculate Your Calorie Needs?

Recalculate when your body weight, activity level, training schedule, or goal changes meaningfully. There is usually little value in recalculating for every small day-to-day weight fluctuation.

For progress tracking, compare measurements under similar conditions and look at trends over weeks rather than focusing on a single day's scale reading.

# Who Should Use a Calorie Calculator?

The calculator is designed for adults who want an estimate of daily energy needs for general nutrition and fitness planning. It can be useful for maintenance, weight-loss, weight-gain, or macro planning.

It is not a diagnostic or medical tool. People who are pregnant or breastfeeding, children and adolescents, and people with medical conditions that materially affect nutrition or metabolism should use condition-specific guidance rather than relying on a general consumer calorie calculator. NIDDK's Body Weight Planner is explicitly intended for adults and excludes younger people and pregnancy or breastfeeding.

# Frequently Asked Questions

## How many calories should I eat per day?

Your daily calorie needs depend on age, sex, height, weight, activity level, and other factors. Use your personalized calculator result as a starting estimate rather than a universal target.

## How many calories should I eat to lose weight?

A common approach is to start below estimated maintenance calories. The appropriate deficit depends on the person and goal, and actual weight change should be monitored over time.

## How do I calculate how many calories I need?

Estimate resting energy with a formula such as Mifflin-St Jeor, account for physical activity, and then adjust the result for your goal.

## What is a calorie intake calculator?

A calorie intake calculator estimates the amount of energy you may need each day based on personal measurements and activity.

## What is a daily calorie calculator?

A daily calorie calculator is another term for a tool that estimates daily energy needs or a practical calorie target.

## How many calories do I need a day?

There is no single number for everyone. Use your age, sex, height, weight, and activity level to produce an individualized estimate.

## How many calories should I eat to maintain my weight?

Your maintenance target is the calorie level expected to keep weight relatively stable under your current routine. Use your calculator result and then compare it with your real-world trend.

## Can I use the calculator for weight loss?

Yes. The calculator can show a calorie target below estimated maintenance, but it should not be treated as a guarantee of a specific weekly weight change.

## Can I use the calculator to gain weight?

Yes. A target above maintenance can provide a starting point for weight gain. The amount should be adjusted based on actual progress and the person's overall nutrition plan.

## What is the difference between BMR and calorie needs?

BMR is the energy your body uses at rest. Daily calorie needs are higher because they include normal movement and activity.

## What is the difference between TDEE and a calorie calculator?

TDEE refers to total daily energy expenditure, while a calorie calculator presents a practical daily calorie requirement or goal using that expenditure estimate.

## Which calorie formula is most accurate?

No predictive equation is perfect for every person. Mifflin-St Jeor is a common default, while Revised Harris-Benedict and Katch-McArdle provide alternatives.

## Should I use Mifflin-St Jeor or Katch-McArdle?

Mifflin-St Jeor is a practical default when you have basic body measurements. Katch-McArdle can use lean body mass, but its output depends on the accuracy of that body-composition estimate.

## How many calories are in one gram of protein?

Protein provides about 4 kcal per gram. Carbohydrate also provides about 4 kcal per gram, while fat provides about 9 kcal per gram.

## How many calories are in a pound of fat?

The traditional 3,500-calorie rule is a rough approximation, not a precise biological conversion or guaranteed weight-loss prediction.

## Why did my weight change even though I ate the same calories?

Water, glycogen, digestive contents, activity changes, and shifts in energy expenditure can all affect short-term scale weight.

## How often should I recalculate my calories?

Recalculate after meaningful changes in body weight, activity, or goals. For most people, daily recalculation is unnecessary.

## Is a calorie calculator accurate?

It can provide a useful estimate, but it does not directly measure your energy expenditure. Real-world results can differ.

## Does age affect calorie needs?

Yes. Calorie needs generally decline with age at the population level, although individual needs vary.

## Do men need more calories than women?

On average, population estimates are higher for men, but an individual's needs depend on body size, age, activity, and other factors.

## Can I use a calorie calculator if I am pregnant or breastfeeding?

A general adult calorie calculator is not the right tool for pregnancy or breastfeeding. Use guidance appropriate to those life stages from a qualified healthcare professional.

## Can teenagers use this calorie calculator?

General adult calorie calculators should not be used as a substitute for age-specific guidance. Children and adolescents have different energy requirements and should use appropriate professional or pediatric guidance.

## Should I eat back calories burned from exercise?

Exercise-calorie estimates are also estimates. Avoid treating tracker or machine calorie numbers as exact; consider overall intake, activity, and trends together.

## Can I use the calorie result with a macro calculator?

Yes. The calorie result can provide the energy target that a Macro Calculator then distributes among protein, carbohydrate, and fat.
